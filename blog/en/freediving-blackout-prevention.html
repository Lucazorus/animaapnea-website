Freediving blackout: why it happens and how to prevent it

A hypoxic blackout rarely announces itself. Understanding what causes it, why hyperventilation and the ascent are so dangerous, and how a buddy turns a blackout into a non-event is the foundation of every safe breath-hold practice.

LM Lucas MassoniFounder of Anima Apnea ·Updated 12 October 2026 ·12 min read

Key points

  • A blackout is a loss of consciousness caused by too little oxygen reaching the brain. It often comes without any warning.
  • The urge to breathe follows CO₂, not oxygen. Hyperventilation lowers CO₂ and switches off the alarm without adding a useful oxygen reserve.
  • In depth diving, the pressure drop on ascent makes lung oxygen fall quickly: the last metres and the first moments at the surface are the riskiest.
  • Never in the water alone: one up, one down, and the buddy watches until the diver has fully recovered. Dry holds only lying down or seated.

Most freedivers who have blacked out describe the same thing: there was no moment of struggle, no feeling of drowning. One second they were finishing a dive or a hold, the next they were coughing at the surface with a buddy holding their head. Some do not remember losing consciousness at all.

That is what makes hypoxic blackout the central safety issue of breath-hold diving. It is not rare among people who push their limits, it does not reliably give warning signs, and in the water it can turn into drowning within moments. The good news is that it is well understood. Its causes are known, its timing is predictable, and the rules that prevent it from becoming an accident are simple. This article explains the mechanism first, because rules are much easier to respect when you understand why they exist.

What a hypoxic blackout is

During a breath-hold, your body keeps consuming oxygen. The brain is the organ most sensitive to a shortage: when the oxygen pressure in arterial blood falls below a critical level, the brain can no longer sustain consciousness and switches off. This is a hypoxic blackout, also called a syncope. It is a protective shutdown of sorts, since an unconscious brain consumes less, but it removes every ability to act.

Out of the water, a blackout during a breath-hold is usually short: once consciousness is lost, the reflex drive to breathe takes over and breathing restarts on its own. In the water, the same reflex becomes the danger. If the airway is under the surface when breathing restarts, water is inhaled. This is why a blackout in the water is an emergency and a blackout on a bed, while never trivial, is usually not.

Trained apneists can tolerate surprisingly low blood oxygen levels during maximal holds3. That tolerance is exactly what makes them vulnerable: the better you become at staying calm while hypoxic, the closer you can get to the threshold without noticing.

Why it often comes without warning

The sensation that ends most breath-holds, the urge to breathe followed by diaphragm contractions, is triggered mainly by the rise of carbon dioxide, detected by chemoreceptors in the brainstem and the carotid arteries2. Your body has a sensitive alarm for CO₂ and a much weaker one for oxygen. Low oxygen on its own produces little discomfort. As it worsens, it tends to dull judgement and awareness rather than create alarm.

In a normal hold, these two timelines are well separated: CO₂ makes you want to breathe long before oxygen becomes critical. Everything that brings them closer together increases the risk. The two classic culprits are hyperventilation before the hold and, in depth diving, the ascent.

Your body has a loud alarm for carbon dioxide and almost none for oxygen. Blackout happens in the gap between the two.

Hyperventilation: the classic trigger

Hyperventilating means breathing faster or deeper than your body needs. It flushes CO₂ out of the blood, sometimes dramatically. What it barely changes is oxygen: at rest, the haemoglobin in your arterial blood is already almost fully saturated, so extra breaths add very little to your store.

The consequence was described by Craig in 1961, in a study of swimmers who lost consciousness underwater1. After hyperventilating, the swimmers started their breath-hold with low CO₂. The urge to breathe arrived much later than usual, but the oxygen supply had not grown accordingly. Oxygen reached a critical level before the CO₂ alarm went off, and they lost consciousness while still feeling able to continue.

The trap is that hyperventilation feels like it works. Your hold gets longer and more comfortable, at least at first. What has actually happened is that you have pushed back the warning and left the danger where it was. This is why every serious freediving school forbids it, and why the preparation breathing taught in the breathwork guide is calm and slow, with a long exhale, rather than fast and forceful.

How to tell you have over-breathed

Tingling in the fingers or around the lips, light-headedness, or a slightly detached feeling before the hold are signs of low CO₂. If you notice them, do not start the hold: breathe normally for a minute or two until they disappear.

Blackout on ascent in depth diving

Depth adds a second mechanism, often called shallow-water blackout. The term has been used for different things over the years, so it is worth describing what actually happens2.

Water pressure increases by roughly one atmosphere every 10 metres. At 10 m, the pressure around you is twice the surface pressure, and the air in your lungs is squeezed to about half its volume. The oxygen in that air is compressed too, so its partial pressure, the push that drives oxygen from the lungs into the blood, rises. At depth, your blood loads oxygen easily, even though you are using it up.

On the way back up, the process reverses. As the pressure drops, the lungs expand and the partial pressure of the remaining oxygen falls, quickly, and fastest in the shallowest part of the ascent, where pressure changes most in relative terms. A diver who felt fine at the bottom can find that the oxygen pressure in the lungs falls below what the brain needs in the last metres or just after reaching the surface. Because blood takes a few moments to travel from the lungs to the brain, the blackout can occur after the diver has surfaced and even taken a first breath.

Two practical conclusions follow. First, feeling fine at depth says little about how you will feel at the surface. Second, the moment a diver surfaces is not the end of the dive: it is one of its most dangerous points. Depth diving should only be learned in a certified course (AIDA, Molchanovs, SSI, PADI Freediver or equivalent), where these mechanisms are taught in the water with qualified instructors.

Loss of motor control: the samba

Between full control and full blackout, there is sometimes an intermediate stage: loss of motor control, nicknamed the samba by freedivers. The diver is still partly conscious but can no longer control their body properly. It can look like trembling or jerky movements of the head and arms, an unfocused gaze, an inability to keep the face out of the water or to complete a simple surface routine.

A samba is a sign of severe hypoxia. It means the diver went too far, and it can progress to a full blackout. The buddy should treat it exactly as they would a blackout: get close, support the head and keep the airway out of the water until the diver has clearly recovered. The diver should end the session.

The risk in dry training

Training out of the water, lying on a bed or a sofa, is the safest way to practise static apnea on your own, and the right environment for CO2 tables and most other exercises. The risk is lower for two reasons: you are usually not pushing to your absolute maximum, and if you do lose consciousness, breathing restarts in the air rather than underwater.

Lower is not zero. A blackout while standing can cause a fall and a head injury. A blackout while driving is obviously catastrophic. And breath-holding in a bath, a pool or any body of water without a trained buddy reproduces exactly the conditions of drowning: there have been fatal accidents during solo breath-hold practice in water, including in baths and pools. Dry training means dry.

!
Non-negotiable rules

Never practise breath-holding in water alone, whatever its depth. On your own, only dry, lying down or seated in a safe place: never standing, driving, in a bath or near water. Never hyperventilate. Stop at the first warning sign. If you have a heart or lung condition, epilepsy, or are pregnant, ask your doctor before any breath-hold training.

The buddy system

Every rule in freediving safety comes back to one idea: blackout itself is rarely what kills, the absence of someone to help is. A diver who blacks out next to an attentive, trained buddy is usually breathing again within moments. The buddy system is how this is organised.

  1. One up, one downOnly one person holds their breath at a time. The other is at the surface, breathing normally, fully focused on the diver, close enough to reach them in seconds.
  2. The buddy knows the planBefore each dive or static, agree on the target time or depth and on a signal. In static, a simple tap and finger signal at regular intervals confirms the diver is responsive.
  3. In depth, the buddy meets the diverThe buddy descends to meet the diver during the last part of the ascent and comes up alongside them, watching their face, because this is where blackout is most likely.
  4. Watch until fully recoveredAfter surfacing, keep watching closely. Many courses teach at least 30 seconds; the real criterion is that the diver is breathing normally, responsive and clearly themselves.
  5. Swap roles only after recoveryThe buddy does not dive until the previous diver has fully recovered and is ready to watch in turn.

A buddy is only useful if they are trained to recognise a problem and respond to it. Holding your breath next to a friend who has no idea what a samba looks like is not a buddy system.

What to do if your buddy blacks out

This section explains the general principle so that you understand what a course will teach you. It is not first-aid training, and reading it does not prepare you to perform a rescue. Rescue techniques have to be learned and practised in the water, with an instructor.

The principle is to protect the airway and help breathing restart. The rescuer brings the diver's face out of the water and keeps it there, supporting the head and keeping the mouth and nose clear. The mask is removed so that the face is exposed to air. Then the classic sequence, often summarised as blow, tap, talk: blowing across the eyes and face, tapping the cheek, and talking to the diver, telling them to breathe. These stimuli often help breathing resume. If breathing does not restart quickly, the situation requires emergency services and the rescue breathing and resuscitation skills taught in a first-aid and freediving rescue course.

After the event

A diver who has blacked out in the water should stop for the day, even if they feel fine. Medical assessment is advised after any blackout in water, and is essential if water may have been inhaled or if coughing, breathlessness or chest discomfort appear afterwards.

Warning signs that sometimes appear

The most important thing to know about warning signs is that they are unreliable. Many blackouts happen without any of them. Still, when they do appear, they must end the hold immediately. For the diver, signs can include:

  • Visual changes: narrowing of vision, darkening, or difficulty focusing.
  • Unusual sensations: tingling, a feeling of warmth or well-being, or a sudden absence of discomfort late in a hard hold.
  • Altered thinking: confusion, losing track of time, or a vague, dream-like state.
  • Loss of coordination: clumsy hands, difficulty performing a simple gesture.

Notice that several of these feel pleasant rather than alarming. That is the problem with hypoxia: it does not frighten you, it disarms you. A buddy may see signs the diver cannot: a vacant expression, a delayed or missing response to a signal, lips turning bluish, small twitches, slowing or stopping of a fin kick, or air escaping from the mouth.

Contractions, by contrast, are not a warning sign of blackout. They are the CO₂ alarm doing its job, as explained in our article on contractions in apnea. But be careful with the opposite conclusion: no contractions does not mean you are safe, especially if you have over-breathed beforehand. In dry training, the Anima Apnea timer lets you note when your first contraction arrives. If it suddenly appears much later than usual, ask yourself how you breathed before the hold.

Frequently asked questions

Can you black out during dry static apnea?

Yes. It is much less likely if you train well below your maximum and never hyperventilate, but it is possible. That is why dry holds are done lying down or seated in a safe place, never standing, driving, in a bath or in any water.

Is a blackout in the water always fatal?

No. With a trained buddy who reaches the diver immediately and keeps the airway out of the water, freedivers usually recover quickly. Alone, the same blackout can lead to drowning. The difference is the buddy.

Is a little hyperventilation acceptable before a hold?

No. Breathe calmly before a hold, then take one full, unforced final breath. If you feel tingling in your fingers or lips, or light-headedness, you have over-breathed: wait and breathe normally before starting.

How long should a buddy keep watching after a dive?

Until the diver is fully recovered: breathing normally, responsive and clearly themselves. Many courses teach a minimum of about 30 seconds after surfacing, because blackout can occur in the first moments at the surface.

Do contractions mean I am about to black out?

No. Contractions are mainly driven by rising CO2, not by a lack of oxygen. But the reverse is also true: having no contractions does not mean you are safe, especially after hyperventilation.

Should I see a doctor after a blackout?

Yes, after any blackout in the water, if water may have been inhaled, or if recovery is not quick and complete. Coughing, breathlessness or chest discomfort in the following hours also need medical attention.
