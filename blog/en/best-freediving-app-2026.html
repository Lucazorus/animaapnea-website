Buyer's guide

How to choose a freediving training app in 2026

Most apps can count seconds. Far fewer help you train. Here are the criteria that separate a genuine training tool from a stopwatch with a nice icon, and how to test them in a week before you commit.

LM Lucas MassoniFounder of Anima Apnea ·Updated 12 October 2026 ·11 min read

Key takeaways

  • The most useful feature is tables built from your own record, not a library of fixed presets.
  • Contraction logging and audio or haptic cues matter more than charts: they let you train with your eyes closed and measure what is really changing.
  • Look for clear safety messaging. An app that encourages hyperventilation or solo water training is a red flag.
  • Check the price model, privacy and platform before you log months of sessions in an app.

Search for a freediving app and you will find timers, breathing apps, table generators and dive-computer companions, all promising to improve your breath-hold. Many of them are perfectly decent. The problem is that they solve different problems, and the one you pick shapes how you train for months.

This guide is written for someone who trains static apnea on land, alone or between pool sessions, and wants a tool that supports a real method. It lists the criteria that matter, explains why, and tells you what to look for in each.

Contractions: the metric most apps ignore

A static hold has an easy phase and a struggle phase, separated by the physiological breaking point: the first involuntary contraction of the diaphragm1. When that first contraction arrives later at the same hold time, your tolerance to carbon dioxide is improving, even if your maximum has not moved yet. Contractions are not a danger signal in themselves; in trained divers they are even associated with better cerebral oxygenation during the struggle phase2. An app that only records total time throws this information away. The guide to apnea contractions explains how to read them.

Cues you can follow with your eyes closed

Relaxation is the whole game in static apnea. If you have to glance at a screen to know when the next hold starts, you stay alert and slightly tense. Good apps announce the start and end of each phase with sound and vibration, so you can lie still, eyes closed, and let the table run.

Breathing work, done properly

Slow breathing with a long exhale is associated with calmer physiological and psychological states3, which is why guided patterns are worth having. What you do not want is an app that mixes freediving with fast, forceful breathing before holds. That is hyperventilation, and it delays the urge to breathe without adding meaningful oxygen4. For the patterns that do help, see the breathwork guide.

What "table generation" should really mean

Plenty of apps offer a beginner, intermediate and advanced CO2 table. That is better than nothing, but a fixed preset cannot know that your record is 2:15 rather than 3:30. A table that is too easy produces no stimulus; one that is too hard turns every session into a fight and teaches tension.

Real table generation starts from your personal best and derives every number from it. For a CO2 table, a common construction keeps the hold at about half your record and shortens the recovery by 15 seconds each round. Move the slider below to see how the whole session changes with the record.

Same rules as the adapted tables in the Anima Apnea app: hold at 50 % of your record and first recovery at two thirds of it but never under 2:00, rounded to 15 s, then −15 s per round (minimum 15 s).

When you evaluate an app, check three things. Does it ask for your record, or let you set it from a timed attempt? Can you edit the result if a round is too hard? And does it let you build an O2 table too, with fixed recovery and increasing holds? The CO2 table guide explains the construction in detail if you prefer to check an app's numbers by hand.

Safety messaging is a feature

An app that coaches breath-holding takes on a responsibility. Loss of consciousness from hypoxia can occur without warning, and in water it can be fatal within moments. The way an app talks about this tells you a lot about whether it was built by people who train.

!
Red flags in any apnea app

Encouraging fast or forced breathing before holds, suggesting holds in the bath or while standing, presenting maximal attempts as daily training, or implying the app makes water training safe without a buddy. Dry training belongs lying down or seated, and nothing replaces a trained buddy in water.

  • Never hyperventilate before a hold, whatever an app's breathing exercise suggests.
  • Stop at dizziness, tingling in hands or lips, visual changes or anything unusual.
  • Never train while driving, in the bath or anywhere a blackout would be dangerous.
  • Ask a doctor first if you have a heart or lung condition, epilepsy, or are pregnant.

How to decide in one week

Feature lists only go so far. The real test is a week of normal training. Here is a simple protocol to judge any app, including ours.

  1. Day 1: set your baselineMeasure your personal best safely, lying down, after a few minutes of calm breathing. Note whether the app makes this easy and stores it. The guide on measuring your personal best covers the method.
  2. Day 2: run a CO2 tableCheck that the table is built from your record, that cues are clear with eyes closed, and that you can mark contractions without breaking concentration.
  3. Day 4: try the breathing toolsUse a slow pattern before a short session. Notice whether the timings are adjustable and the guidance calm.
  4. Day 6: run a second table and read the historyCan you compare both sessions, including first-contraction times? If not, the app will not show you progress later.
  5. Day 7: check the boring partsWhat is free, what is paid, whether an account is required, and whether you can trust it with months of data.

If you are just starting out, pairing the app you choose with a structured block helps more than any feature. The 4-week beginner program gives you one, day by day.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need an app to train static apnea?

No. A stopwatch, a pencil and a printed table are enough to start. An app becomes useful when it removes friction: building tables from your record, timing every round with cues so you can close your eyes, and keeping a history you can actually learn from.

Is a free app good enough?

Often, yes. What matters is whether the free version lets you time holds, run a table built from your record and keep your history. Check what sits behind a paywall before you invest weeks of data in an app.

Can an app replace a freediving course or a buddy?

No. An app can structure dry training on land. Anything you do in water requires a trained buddy watching you, and a certified course is the right way to learn in-water technique and rescue.

Does Anima Apnea work on Android or measure heart rate?

No. Anima Apnea is an iPhone app. It uses no sensors and does not measure heart rate or blood oxygen saturation. It times your holds and tables, records the contractions you tap, and tracks your progress.

Should I switch apps if mine already works?

Probably not. If your current app lets you train consistently and safely, consistency is worth more than any feature. Switch only if a missing criterion is genuinely holding back your training.
