The breathe-up: how to prepare for a static hold

The two or three minutes before a hold shape everything that follows. Done well, the breathe-up leaves you calm, heavy and ready. Done badly, it turns into hidden hyperventilation. Here is how to get it right.

LM Lucas MassoniFounder of Anima Apnea ·Updated 12 October 2026 ·11 min read

Key takeaways

  • The breathe-up is about relaxation, a calmer heart and normal CO₂, not about loading oxygen.
  • Breathe slowly with an exhale about twice as long as the inhale (for example 4 s in, 8 s out) for 2 to 3 minutes.
  • Use the time for a body scan, then take a final breath that is full but not forced, around 85–90 % of your maximum.
  • Dizziness or tingling means you are over-breathing: stop and start again more gently.

Watch an experienced freediver before a static attempt and you will see very little. They lie still, eyes closed, breathing so slowly and quietly that you might think they had fallen asleep. Then a single long exhale, one unhurried inhale, and the hold begins. That apparent stillness is the breathe-up, and it is one of the most underrated skills in breath-hold training.

Beginners often do the opposite: big, fast breaths to “fill up” before the hold. It feels like preparation, but it works against them and adds risk. This guide explains what the breathe-up is for, how to breathe during it, how to take the final breath, and gives you a simple protocol you can use before every dry hold.

What the breathe-up is for

A static hold depends on three things: how much oxygen you store, how slowly you use it, and how well you tolerate the rising urge to breathe12. The breathe-up cannot change the first one much. At rest, your blood is already close to fully saturated with oxygen, so extra breaths add almost nothing.

What it can change is the state you start in. A good breathe-up has four goals:

  • Relaxation. Loose muscles and a quiet mind consume less oxygen, from the very first second of the hold.
  • A calmer heart. Slow breathing with a long exhale shifts the nervous system toward its rest-and-digest mode3.
  • Normal CO₂. You want to start with a normal level of carbon dioxide, so that the urge to breathe still warns you at the right time.
  • A routine. The same sequence before every hold becomes a cue for your body to settle, and makes your sessions comparable.

A breathe-up is not hyperventilation

Hyperventilation means breathing more than your body needs, faster or deeper. It lowers CO₂ well below normal. Since CO₂ is what triggers the urge to breathe, that urge is delayed, and oxygen can fall dangerously low before you feel any need to stop. This is the classic mechanism behind blackouts in breath-hold swimmers4, explained in more detail in our guide to preventing freediving blackout.

The 1:2 rhythm

The simplest effective breathe-up is slow diaphragmatic breathing with an exhale about twice as long as the inhale. A 4-second inhale and an 8-second exhale give five breaths a minute, which sits in the slow-breathing range that research associates with greater relaxation and parasympathetic activity3.

The long exhale matters because your heart naturally speeds up slightly as you breathe in and slows as you breathe out. Giving the exhale more time tilts each cycle toward the calming side. If 4/8 feels tight, use 3/6; the ratio matters more than the exact count.

Keep the breaths only slightly larger than normal. Slow does not mean huge: very large breaths, even at a slow pace, can still wash out too much CO₂. Breathing through the nose makes the rhythm easier to control, and you can let the exhale escape through softly pursed lips. The same pattern is covered among other techniques in our guide to breathwork for freediving.

Posture and body scan

For dry training, lie on your back on a bed, a mat or a sofa, with a small cushion under the head and, if you like, under the knees. Arms rest by your sides, palms open. The aim is a position you could hold for several minutes without adjusting anything. Sitting in a reclined chair also works; standing never does.

Once the rhythm is established, use each exhale to release one area of the body. A simple order, from top to bottom:

  • Face: forehead, eyes, jaw slightly open, tongue soft.
  • Neck and shoulders: let the shoulders sink toward the floor.
  • Arms and hands: fingers loose, no fists.
  • Belly: soft, so the diaphragm can move freely.
  • Legs and feet: heavy, turned slightly outward.

Keep returning to the jaw and shoulders: they are where tension comes back first, both during the breathe-up and during the hold.

The final breath

The last breath before the hold deserves its own attention. Start with a slow, complete exhale, without squeezing. Then inhale smoothly, filling the belly first, then the lower ribs, then the chest. Stop at around 85–90 % of your maximum: full, but without the last effortful sips that tense the neck and shoulders. Close the airway gently, relax the shoulders once more and start the hold.

Taking a forced 100 % breath is tempting but often counterproductive. The extra volume is small, and the tension it creates makes the early part of the hold less comfortable and costs oxygen.

A word on lung packing

Some advanced freedivers add lung packing (glossopharyngeal insufflation) after the final breath, using the mouth and throat to pump extra air into already full lungs. It increases lung volume, but it has documented risks, including fainting caused by a drop in blood pressure and lung over-expansion injuries1. It is a technique for experienced athletes taught by a qualified instructor. This guide deliberately does not teach it, and you do not need it to progress well beyond your current personal best.

A three-minute protocol, step by step

Here is a complete breathe-up for a single dry static hold, such as a personal best measurement. The timeline shows how the three minutes are divided.

  1. Set up safely, on dry landLie down in a quiet room. Prepare your timer, put the phone in do-not-disturb mode and make sure nothing can interrupt you.
  2. Settle for about 30 secondsLet your breathing find its own pace. Adjust your position once, then stop moving.
  3. Slow 1:2 breathing for about two minutesBreathe in through the nose for 4 seconds, belly first, and out for 8 seconds without pushing. Keep each breath only slightly larger than normal.
  4. Body scan on the exhalesRelease the face, jaw, shoulders, hands, belly and legs, one area per exhale. Come back to the jaw and shoulders often.
  5. Exhale, then take the final breathOne slow, complete exhale, then a smooth inhale from belly to chest, stopping around 85–90 % of your maximum.
  6. Start the hold and stay softStart the timer, release the shoulders one last time and keep scanning for tension. After the hold, take at least three recovery breaths before anything else.

Between the holds of a CO₂ table, there is no time for a full three-minute sequence: the recovery period is your breathe-up. Keep the same slow, exhale-led rhythm for as long as the recovery allows, and finish with the same final breath.

Safety comes before any protocol

Do dry holds alone only lying down or sitting, never standing, driving or in the bath. Never hold your breath in water alone: a blackout can happen without warning and can be fatal within moments. In water, train only with a trained buddy watching you, ideally after a recognised freediving course. Stop if you feel dizzy or tingly, never hyperventilate, and ask a doctor first if you have a heart or lung condition, epilepsy, or are pregnant.

Common mistakes

  • Breathing too big. The most common error. Slow but very deep breaths still lower CO₂. If you feel light-headed, you are over-breathing.
  • Making it longer and longer. More is not better. Beyond a few minutes, many people drift into larger breaths without noticing.
  • Forcing the final breath. Gasping for the last few percent tenses the neck and shoulders just as the hold begins.
  • Skipping the body scan. Breathing slowly while your jaw and fists are clenched only does half the job.
  • Changing the routine every time. A stable sequence lets you compare holds and teaches your body to settle on cue.
  • Watching the clock. Let a timer guide the phases so your attention stays on your body.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a breathe-up last?

Two to three minutes before a single hold or the first hold of a session. Between the holds of a table, the recovery period is your breathe-up, so simply keep the same calm rhythm for as long as it lasts.

Is it normal to feel dizzy or tingly after the breathe-up?

No. Light-headedness or tingling in the fingers or lips means you have been over-breathing. Do not start the hold: breathe normally for a few minutes, then begin again with smaller, slower breaths.

Why not fill the lungs to 100 % for the last breath?

Forcing the last few percent tenses the chest, neck and shoulders, which makes the hold less comfortable and costs oxygen. A full but relaxed breath, around 85 to 90 % of your maximum, usually gives a better hold.

Should I learn lung packing?

Not as a beginner or intermediate. Lung packing carries real risks, including fainting and lung over-expansion injuries. It is reserved for experienced athletes taught by a qualified instructor, and you can make excellent progress without it.

Can I do the breathe-up sitting down?

Yes, sitting or lying down both work, but never standing. For dry holds on your own, lying down is the safest position because a loss of consciousness cannot lead to a fall.
