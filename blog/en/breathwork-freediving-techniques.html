Breathwork for freediving: the techniques that actually help

Most breathing advice for freedivers mixes the useful with the risky. Here is what slow, diaphragmatic and structured breathing really do for a static hold, how to practise them, and which popular methods to keep away from your training.

LM Lucas MassoniFounder of Anima Apnea ·Updated 12 October 2026 ·11 min read

Key takeaways

  • Breathing before a hold cannot add much oxygen. It helps by relaxing you and keeping your CO₂ at a normal level.
  • Diaphragmatic breathing and slow breathing around 6 breaths a minute are the foundation; a long exhale is the main relaxation lever.
  • Box breathing and 4-7-8 are useful tools for focus and calm, not CO₂ training.
  • Never hyperventilate before a hold, and keep power-breathing methods well away from breath-holds and water.

Ask ten freedivers how to breathe before a static hold and you will get ten rituals. Some are excellent. Others, such as long series of fast, deep breaths, feel powerful but quietly remove the safety signal that tells you when to stop. The difference matters far more than any pattern or count.

This guide sorts the techniques by what they actually do. You will find the foundations (diaphragmatic and slow breathing), two structured patterns that help you focus (box breathing and 4-7-8), the recovery breath every freediver should know, and a clear explanation of why hyperventilation and power-breathing methods do not belong before a hold.

What breathing can and cannot do for a hold

A static hold depends on three things: how much oxygen you carry, how slowly you use it, and how well you tolerate the rising urge to breathe1. Breathing technique has surprisingly little effect on the first. At rest, your haemoglobin is already close to fully saturated, so extra breaths add almost nothing to your oxygen store.

Where breathing does make a difference is in the other two. A slow, relaxed breathing pattern calms the nervous system, softens muscle tension and lowers your oxygen consumption before and during the hold. And breathing calmly, rather than forcefully, means you start the hold with a normal level of carbon dioxide, so the urge to breathe arrives when it should. That urge is driven by CO₂, not by low oxygen, which is why it is so dangerous to manipulate (the CO₂ table guide explains the mechanism in detail).

Good breathwork does not load you with oxygen. It makes you quieter, so the oxygen you already have lasts longer.

Diaphragmatic breathing: the foundation

The diaphragm is your main breathing muscle. When it contracts, it moves down and the belly gently expands; when it relaxes, air flows out with very little effort. Many adults breathe mostly with the upper chest, which is shallower and recruits neck and shoulder muscles that have no business being tense before a hold.

Diaphragmatic breathing re-centres the breath low in the body. In an eight-week study, healthy adults who trained diaphragmatic breathing showed better sustained attention, less negative affect and lower salivary cortisol than a control group2. For a freediver, it also builds awareness of the diaphragm itself, which helps later when contractions arrive.

How to practise it

  • Lie on your back, knees bent if that is more comfortable, one hand on the belly and one on the chest.
  • Inhale through the nose for about 4 seconds, letting the belly rise first while the chest stays fairly still.
  • Exhale slowly for about 8 seconds, without pushing, and let the belly fall back.
  • Continue for 5 to 10 minutes. If the 4/8 rhythm feels tight, start at 3/6 and lengthen over the following days.

Slow breathing around six breaths a minute

Slow-paced breathing, generally defined as fewer than ten breaths a minute and often around six, is one of the best-studied breathing interventions. A systematic review found it consistently associated with increased heart rate variability and parasympathetic activity, along with greater comfort, relaxation and lower anxiety3. That is exactly the state you want to carry into a hold.

You do not need a particular count to get there. A 5 s in / 5 s out rhythm gives six breaths a minute; the 4/8 diaphragmatic pattern gives five. What matters is that the breath stays smooth, quiet and well below your normal resting rate, without any sense of air hunger.

Why the exhale matters most

Your heart rate naturally rises slightly as you breathe in and falls as you breathe out. A longer exhale therefore spends more of each cycle on the calming side. This is the logic behind exhale-focused breathing: keep the inhale easy and let the exhale last roughly twice as long, as in the 1:2 ratio of the 4/8 pattern. It is also the backbone of a good breathe-up before a hold.

✓
Slow is not the same as deep

Slow breathing works because the pace drops, not because each breath becomes huge. If you start making very large breaths at a slow pace, you can still blow off too much CO₂. Keep each breath comfortable, only slightly larger than normal.

Box breathing and 4-7-8

Structured patterns give the mind something precise to follow, which is often what a restless beginner needs. The two most common ones are worth knowing, as long as you understand what they are for.

Box breathing uses four equal phases: inhale 4 s, hold 4 s, exhale 4 s, hold 4 s. It is a concentration exercise: the symmetry makes it easy to notice when your attention drifts. Its short pauses are not long enough to train CO₂ tolerance in any meaningful way.

4-7-8, popularised by Andrew Weil from yoga breathing practices, combines a 4 s inhale, a 7 s pause and an 8 s exhale. The long exhale makes it strongly calming, and a full cycle lasts 19 seconds, so the pace is very slow. A few cycles are usually enough; if the pause makes you uncomfortable, shorten every phase while keeping the proportions.

Recovery breathing after every hold

The breaths that follow a hold matter as much as the ones before it. Oxygen levels keep falling for a few seconds after you start breathing again, because fresh air takes time to reach the blood. Recovery breathing, sometimes called hook breathing, is taught on every recognised freediving course for that reason.

  • Exhale only briefly, without emptying the lungs.
  • Inhale quickly and fully.
  • Hold for one or two seconds with a light pressure, as if saying a silent “hook”.
  • Release and repeat at least three times, then return to calm breathing.

Practise it after every dry hold, including easy ones, until it becomes automatic. It is the reflex you want available on the day a hold goes further than planned.

What to avoid before a hold

Hyperventilation means breathing faster or deeper than your body needs. It feels like preparation, which is what makes it treacherous. Because blood oxygen is already near its maximum, over-breathing adds very little oxygen. What it does change is CO₂: it washes it out and lowers it well below normal. Since CO₂ is what triggers the urge to breathe, the alarm is pushed back, and your oxygen can fall to a dangerous level before you feel any need to stop. This mechanism was identified decades ago as a cause of loss of consciousness in underwater swimmers4.

Signs that you are over-breathing include light-headedness, tingling in the fingers or lips, and a strange sense of ease at the start of the hold. If you notice any of them, stop, breathe normally for a few minutes and start again more gently.

Power-breathing methods

Methods built on rounds of deep, rapid breaths followed by a hold, the best known being Wim Hof-style breathing, are hyperventilation by design. Whatever their other merits, they produce exactly the delayed alarm shown above. Their promoters themselves warn against practising them in or near water. Keep them on separate days from your apnea training, never chain them with static holds, and never use them before any time in the water.

!
Breathing practice is a dry-land activity

Practise breathing patterns and dry holds lying down or sitting, never standing, driving or in the bath. Never hold your breath in water alone: a blackout can occur without warning and becomes fatal within moments. In water, train only with a trained buddy watching you the whole time. Stop if you feel dizzy, tingly or unwell, and ask a doctor before training if you have a heart or lung condition, epilepsy, or are pregnant.

A simple weekly routine

Breathing practice works through repetition, not intensity. A few minutes most days will change your default breathing more than one long session a week. The routine below fits around two CO₂ tables and one O₂ table; adapt the days to your schedule.

Frequently asked questions

Does breathwork increase the oxygen I start a hold with?

Barely. At rest, your blood is already almost fully saturated with oxygen. Good breathing helps in other ways: it relaxes you, so you use oxygen more slowly, and it lets you start the hold with normal CO2 instead of an artificially low level.

Is box breathing a form of CO2 training?

Only very mildly. Its four-second holds are far too short to build real CO2 tolerance. Treat it as a focus and regularity exercise, and use CO2 tables for tolerance work.

Can I do Wim Hof-style breathing before a static hold?

No. Rounds of power breathing are a form of hyperventilation: they delay the urge to breathe without adding meaningful oxygen, which raises the risk of blacking out. Keep them completely separate from breath-hold training, and never do them in or near water.

How long should I practise breathing each day?

Five to ten minutes is enough. Regular short sessions build the habit of calm, diaphragmatic breathing far better than an occasional long one.

Should I breathe through my nose or my mouth?

For slow breathing, the nose makes the pace easier to control, and a long exhale through softly pursed lips works well. What matters most is a slow, quiet, relaxed rhythm, whichever airway you use.
