Contractions in apnea: what they are and how to handle them

Sooner or later, every breath-hold reaches the moment when your diaphragm starts to jerk on its own. Understanding these contractions changes how you experience them, and turns them into one of the most useful measures of your progress.

LM Lucas MassoniFounder of Anima Apnea ·Updated 12 October 2026 ·11 min read

Key points

  • Contractions are involuntary breathing movements: your respiratory centre trying to breathe against a closed airway.
  • The first one marks the physiological breaking point, the end of the easy phase and the start of the struggle phase.
  • They are driven mainly by rising CO₂. They are not an oxygen gauge, and research in elite divers suggests they may even help brain oxygenation.
  • Your first-contraction time, measured in comparable conditions, is a simple and reliable way to follow your CO₂ tolerance.

The first time it happens, it is unsettling. You are holding your breath, calm, and suddenly your belly jerks inward, your throat tightens, and the jerk comes back a few seconds later, stronger. It feels like your body is staging a revolt. Many beginners stop there, convinced that something has gone wrong.

Nothing has gone wrong. Contractions are one of the most predictable events in a breath-hold, and understanding them is one of the biggest mental steps in static apnea. This article explains what they are, what triggers them, why they do not mean you are running out of oxygen, how to stay relaxed through them, and how to use them as a training metric.

What contractions are

In the scientific literature, contractions are called involuntary breathing movements. They are rhythmic contractions of the diaphragm, and to a lesser extent of the other inspiratory muscles, while your airway stays closed1. Your brainstem's respiratory centre sends the command to breathe, the muscles respond, but no air moves. You feel it as a jolt in the abdomen or the lower chest, sometimes with a reflex tightening of the throat.

The main trigger is chemical. During a hold, CO₂ accumulates in your blood and makes it more acidic. Chemoreceptors in the brainstem and the carotid arteries detect this and increase the drive to breathe. Falling oxygen adds to this drive later in the hold, and lung volume plays a role too: holds started on a full breath generally reach the breaking point later than holds on a partial breath1. When the drive becomes strong enough, it overrides your voluntary control of the diaphragm, and the contractions begin.

The breaking point: easy phase and struggle phase

Research describes a long breath-hold in two parts1. First comes the easy-going phase, during which there is little or no discomfort. The first involuntary breathing movement marks the physiological breaking point. After it comes the struggle phase, in which contractions repeat, become more frequent and usually stronger, until you decide to breathe. That decision is sometimes called the conventional breaking point, to distinguish it from the physiological one.

In beginners, the struggle phase is often short, because the first contractions feel like a reason to stop. In trained apneists it can make up a large share of the total hold2. The ability to tolerate the combination of high CO₂ and falling oxygen is recognised as one of the main factors of static apnea performance, alongside oxygen stores and the diving response4. In other words, a large part of progress in static apnea happens after the first contraction.

Why contractions are not an oxygen alarm

The intuitive interpretation of a contraction is: I am running out of air. The physiology says otherwise. Because contractions are driven mainly by CO₂, the first one usually arrives while blood oxygen saturation is still high, as Figure 1 illustrates. The signal says that CO₂ is rising, not that oxygen is about to run out.

There is even evidence that contractions can be useful. In a study of elite breath-hold divers, Dujic and colleagues found that involuntary breathing movements during the struggle phase were associated with improved cerebral oxygenation3. The swings in chest pressure they create seem to help blood circulation, a little like a pump. This was observed in elite divers, so it should not be over-interpreted, but it reinforces a simple idea: contractions are not your enemy.

A contraction tells you that CO₂ is rising. It does not tell you that oxygen is running out, and it does not tell you that you are safe.

That last point matters just as much. Contractions are not a safety gauge in either direction. Their absence does not mean you have plenty of oxygen: if you hyperventilate before a hold, you flush out CO₂ and the contractions arrive much later, while your oxygen reserve has barely changed. This is exactly the mechanism behind many blackouts, explained in detail in our article on blackout prevention.

How to handle contractions

Contractions are reflexes. You cannot stop them, but you have a lot of control over how much they cost you and how you experience them. Here are the approaches that work best in dry static training.

  • Expect them and name them. A contraction you were waiting for is far less disturbing than one that surprises you. When it comes, note it mentally, without judgement: there it is.
  • Let the belly move. The instinct is to brace the abdomen against the jolt. Do the opposite: keep the belly soft and let the diaphragm move. Fighting it adds muscle tension and makes each contraction feel stronger.
  • Scan for tension. Contractions tend to drag other muscles along: jaw, shoulders, hands, forehead. After each one, check these areas and let them go. Relaxed muscles consume less oxygen.
  • Try a swallow. Some practitioners find that a gentle swallow eases the throat and takes the edge off a contraction. It does not work for everyone, but it costs nothing to test in dry training.
  • Give your mind a task. Counting the contractions, following a slow mental count or visualising a familiar place keeps attention away from the discomfort.
  • Set your limit before the hold. Decide in advance when you will stop, for example after a set time or a set number of contractions. The decision is calmer before the hold than in the middle of the struggle phase.

What about releasing a little air?

Some experienced static athletes let out a small amount of air late in a hold to ease contractions. It also reduces the volume of air in your lungs, and with it your oxygen reserve. It is not a beginner technique and has no place in depth diving. For dry training on your own, stick to relaxation.

The best way to practise all of this is the CO2 table: its short recoveries bring contractions on in the later rounds, at a hold duration that stays well below your maximum. You get to rehearse relaxing through contractions many times per session, in a controlled setting.

First-contraction time as a training metric

Your maximum hold is a noisy number. It depends on your mood, your motivation, how hard you are willing to push that day, and you should not test it often. The time of your first contraction is much more objective: it happens to you, whether you are motivated or not. Measured in comparable conditions, it is one of the best indicators of how your CO₂ tolerance evolves.

How the onset shifts with training

With regular practice, two things usually change. The first contraction tends to come later for a given type of hold, and the contractions that follow feel less overwhelming. Part of this is physiological adaptation to high CO₂, and part is learned: once you know a contraction is harmless, it stops triggering the stress response that made it feel unbearable2. How fast and how far the onset moves varies a lot from one person to another, which is why your own curve is the only one worth watching.

To make that curve meaningful, keep conditions comparable: same position, similar time of day, same preparation and the same kind of final breath. Avoid holds right after a meal. Expect day-to-day noise from sleep, stress and fatigue, and look at the trend over several weeks rather than at individual sessions. A good rhythm is two or three CO₂ sessions a week, combined occasionally with an O2 table.

✓
A sudden jump is suspicious

Progress in first-contraction time is gradual. If yours suddenly arrives much later than usual, check your preparation before celebrating: faster or deeper breathing before the hold lowers CO₂ and delays contractions without adding any real reserve.

Contractions and safety

Learning to tolerate contractions is the core of static apnea training, which is exactly why it must be done in a safe setting. Staying calm through the struggle phase brings you closer to your physiological limits, and a blackout can occur without any warning.

!
Train contractions on dry land only

On your own, practise only dry, lying down or seated in a safe place: never standing, driving, in a bath or in any water. Never hyperventilate. In the water, only with a trained buddy watching you the whole time. Stop the hold at once if you notice tingling, visual changes, confusion or any unusual sensation. Ask your doctor first if you have a heart or lung condition, epilepsy, or are pregnant.

Frequently asked questions

Are contractions dangerous?

No. They are a normal reflex response to rising CO2, and they are not a sign that you are about to run out of oxygen. What matters for safety is where you train: dry, lying down, or in water only with a trained buddy.

Why do my contractions start earlier on some days?

Fatigue, stress, a recent meal, poor sleep or a rushed preparation can all bring them forward. Compare sessions done in similar conditions, and look at the trend over several weeks rather than at a single day.

Can I stop contractions from happening?

Not safely. The only quick way to delay them a lot is hyperventilation, which removes the CO2 alarm and raises the risk of blackout. Training does push them later and makes them easier to tolerate, gradually.

How many contractions should I accept in training?

There is no universal number. In dry CO2 tables, the last rounds usually include some contractions. Decide your limit before the hold, stay well relaxed, and stop at once if any unusual sensation appears.

Is it normal to have no contractions at all?

On short holds, yes. On long holds, an absence of contractions is more often the result of over-breathing before the hold. That is not a gift: it means your main warning signal has been switched off.
