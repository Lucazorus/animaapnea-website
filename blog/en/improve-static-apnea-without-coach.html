Self-coached static apnea

Improve your static apnea without a coach: structure, feedback and safety, done right

A coach gives you three things: a plan, an outside eye and a safety net. Training alone means rebuilding each of them on purpose. Here is how to do it, and where self-coaching has to stop.

LM Lucas MassoniFounder of Anima Apnea ·Updated 12 October 2026 ·11 min read

Key takeaways

  • A coach provides structure, feedback and safety. Each one can be rebuilt for dry training, but only deliberately.
  • Judge progress with objective markers: first-contraction time and completion at a fixed table, plus how hard the last rounds feel.
  • Keep a log, review it weekly, and plan one lighter week every three to four weeks.
  • The hard limit: self-coaching is for dry training only. Never hold your breath in water alone.

Most people who train static apnea do it alone, on a bed or a yoga mat, between work and dinner. Freediving clubs are rare outside coastal cities, and even certified freedivers spend most of their dry training without anyone watching. That is not a problem in itself. Static apnea is a quiet, internal discipline, and much of the progress comes from repetition, relaxation and patience.

The problem is what goes missing. Without a coach, beginners tend to do the same few things: they test their maximum too often, they train too hard every day, they judge sessions by mood, and they quietly drift towards habits that are unsafe. This guide breaks down what a coach really brings and shows how to replace each part with something concrete you can do on your own.

What a coach actually gives you

Watch a good freediving instructor run a dry session and you will see much more than someone holding a stopwatch. They decide what you do today based on what you did last week. They notice when your shoulders creep up or your breathing becomes noisy. They tell you to stop when you would have kept going, and occasionally to keep going when you would have stopped. And they are physically there if something goes wrong.

Those roles fall into three groups. Once you name them, you can replace each one with a tool rather than with willpower.

Structure: decide the week before it starts

The most common self-training mistake is choosing the session on the day. When you feel good you push, when you feel tired you skip, and after a month you have a pile of unrelated efforts. A coach removes that decision. You can do the same by writing your week down before it begins and sticking to it unless a clear warning sign appears.

A simple, robust structure for dry static uses two kinds of tables. The CO2 table keeps the hold fixed at about half your personal best and shortens the recovery each round, training tolerance to the urge to breathe. The O2 table keeps recovery fixed and lengthens the holds, getting you used to longer apneas. Around them, lighter days of slow breathing or complete rest let the work settle.

A few rules make this structure work:

  • Build every table from a recent personal best. Tables are percentages of that number, so a sloppy measurement skews everything. The protocol is detailed in how to measure your static personal best.
  • Change one thing at a time. If you lengthen the holds, keep the recoveries. If you add a round, keep the holds. Otherwise you cannot tell what caused the result.
  • Keep the same table for at least two weeks. Your markers only mean something if the stimulus stays identical.
  • Train at roughly the same time of day. Digestion, fatigue and stress shift your holds more than you might expect.

If you are unsure how to balance the two tables, the CO2 versus O2 comparison explains when each one is the better choice.

Feedback: three markers that do not lie

A coach's outside eye is mostly about seeing what you cannot feel accurately in the moment. Mood is a poor gauge: a session can feel terrible after a bad night and still be a good one. You need markers that stay comparable across weeks. Three are enough.

First-contraction time at a fixed table

A static hold has an easy phase followed by a struggle phase that starts with the first involuntary contraction of the diaphragm, often called the physiological breaking point1. In trained apneists, the struggle phase can make up a large share of the total hold2. The moment that first contraction appears is a sensitive marker of how well you tolerate rising CO₂. Run the same table, note when the first contraction arrives in a given round, and compare week to week. Later is better.

Completion and hold time at a fixed table

Did you finish every hold as planned, and in which round did you have to cut one short, if any? A table you could not complete two weeks ago and now finish cleanly is progress, even if your maximum has not been retested yet.

How a hard round feels

After the last round, rate the effort from 1 to 10. On its own this number is noisy. Next to the two objective markers, it tells you whether you are adapting (same table, lower rating) or accumulating fatigue (same table, rising rating).

✓
Read the trend, not the session

Any single session can be off by a few seconds for reasons that have nothing to do with training: a late meal, a poor night, a stressful day. Look at three or four sessions of the same table before drawing a conclusion. A coach does exactly this, and it is the habit that protects you from both false hope and false discouragement.

The training log: your coach's notebook

A coach remembers. You will not, at least not reliably. After a month, sessions blur together and you remember the best one and the worst one. A log fixes that, and it does not need to be elaborate. For each session, write down:

  • the date, time of day and type of session;
  • the table used (holds, recoveries, number of rounds);
  • the first-contraction time in a reference round, and the number of contractions if you count them;
  • whether every hold was completed, and if not, which one was cut short;
  • your 1 to 10 rating of the hardest round;
  • one line of context: sleep, meal, stress, anything unusual.

Then, once a week, spend five minutes reading the last few entries and ask three questions. Are the markers stable, improving or getting worse? Was there anything unusual about the bad sessions? Does next week's plan stay the same, get a little harder, or get lighter? That is the whole loop.

A coach is mostly a memory and a brake. A log and a fixed plan give you both.

An app can take over much of the bookkeeping. A paper notebook works too. What matters is that the record exists and that you read it.

Deload weeks and retests

Training stresses the system; adaptation happens in the days that follow. Coaches build that rhythm into the calendar because athletes left alone almost never take enough easy time. Studies of short apnea training periods, including two weeks of daily breath-holds in people new to the practice, show that the body does respond to regular training3, but the response is gradual and varies from person to person4. Pushing harder every week does not speed it up.

In practice, a deload week keeps the habit but cuts the stress: one table instead of two, a couple of rounds fewer, slightly longer recoveries, more slow-breathing sessions. You should finish it feeling rested and a little impatient. That is the right moment to retest your personal best, then recalculate your tables from the new number.

Retesting every two to four weeks is enough. Testing your maximum every few days is one of the classic self-coached errors: maximal attempts are tiring, the day-to-day noise hides any real trend, and the constant pressure turns every session into a verdict. The training plans in Anima Apnea follow the same logic, with four-week blocks and record attempts placed at the end of weeks 2 and 4 rather than every session.

Warning signs that you are doing too much

Without a coach, nobody tells you to back off. These signs are worth taking seriously, especially when two or more appear together:

  • Your markers go backwards. First contractions arrive earlier at the same table over several sessions, or you stop completing rounds you used to finish.
  • The same table feels harder every week. Rising effort ratings without any change in the table usually mean fatigue, not weakness.
  • You start to dread sessions. Static should feel demanding but calm. Anxiety before a session is a signal to lighten the plan.
  • Your sleep or general energy drops during a period of more training.
  • Headaches, unusual dizziness or tingling after sessions. These deserve a pause, and medical advice if they persist.

The response is simple: take three or four easy days, then restart at the previous, easier version of your table. If the signs come back quickly, move the deload week forward.

On lighter days, slow breathing with a long exhale is a good use of the time. Guided patterns such as diaphragmatic breathing or box breathing are covered in our breathwork guide. They keep the habit going without adding load.

The hard limit, and when to find a course or a club

Everything above concerns dry training. That is not a detail: it is the reason self-coaching works at all. On land, lying down, a hold that goes too far ends with you breathing again. In water, the same event can be fatal.

!
Never hold your breath in water alone

A hypoxic blackout can happen without warning, even on a hold that feels comfortable. In water, it can kill within moments. Train alone only on dry land, lying down or seated. Any breath-hold in water, including in a bath or a pool, requires a trained buddy watching you the whole time.

  • Never hyperventilate before a hold. Fast, deep breathing lowers CO₂ and delays the urge to breathe without adding meaningful oxygen. You lose your warning signal, which is the classic path to blackout5.
  • Stop the session if you feel dizzy, notice tingling in your lips or hands, visual changes or anything unusual.
  • Never train while driving, standing, in the bath, or anywhere a loss of consciousness would be dangerous.
  • Get medical advice first if you have a heart or lung condition, epilepsy, or are pregnant.

Self-coaching also has a ceiling. Find a certified course (AIDA, Molchanovs, SSI, PADI Freediver) or a local club when:

  • you want to do any breath-hold in water, whether static in a pool or depth in open water;
  • you want to learn equalization, safety and rescue skills, which need an instructor in person;
  • your markers have been flat for several months despite a sensible plan, and an outside eye could spot what you cannot;
  • you have ever had symptoms that worried you during or after a hold.

Clubs are also where you find training partners, and with them the possibility of supervised pool static. Dry self-coaching and a club or course are not opposites. The best progress usually comes from both.

Frequently asked questions

Can you really improve static apnea without a coach?

Yes, for dry static training. Most of the work happens lying down at home, and a clear plan, a few objective metrics and an honest log cover much of what a coach brings. What you cannot replace alone is supervision in water and hands-on technique correction.

How often should I train static apnea on my own?

Three to four sessions a week is a good range for most people: two table sessions at least 48 hours apart, plus lighter breathing or relaxation work. Plan one lighter week every three to four weeks.

What is the best metric to track my progress?

The time of your first contraction during an identical table. If it arrives later from one week to the next at the same holds and recoveries, your tolerance is improving, even before your personal best moves.

How do I know if I am overtraining?

Watch for first contractions arriving earlier at the same table over several sessions, hard rounds that feel harder every week, poor sleep, low motivation or headaches after sessions. Take a few easy days, and stop and seek medical advice if a symptom is unusual or persists.

When should I take a freediving course?

Before any breath-hold training in water, and whenever you want to learn depth, equalization or rescue skills. A certified course (AIDA, Molchanovs, SSI, PADI Freediver) or a club also helps if you have plateaued for months.
