Breath-hold physiology

The mammalian dive reflex: how your body saves oxygen

Hold your breath and something switches on by itself: the heart slows, blood retreats from the limbs, the spleen squeezes. Here is what the dive reflex really does in humans, what triggers it, how far training can change it, and how to work with it on dry land.

LM Lucas MassoniFounder of Anima Apnea ·Updated 12 October 2026 ·11 min read

Key takeaways

  • The dive reflex combines a slower heart, constricted blood vessels in the limbs and a contracting spleen. All three help you spend oxygen more slowly.
  • A breath-hold alone triggers it. Cold water on the face, particularly the forehead and around the eyes, usually strengthens it.
  • In humans the response is real but moderate compared with seals or whales, and it varies widely from person to person.
  • It helps, but it never protects you from blackout. Dry training alone, lying down; in water, always with a trained buddy.

Put your face in a sink of cold water and hold your breath for half a minute, and your pulse will usually drop. You did not decide that. It is an automatic, ancient reflex shared by every air-breathing mammal that dives, from otters to sperm whales, and humans have kept a modest version of it.

For a freediver, the dive reflex is one of the reasons a well-relaxed breath-hold can last far longer than a simple calculation of oxygen stores would suggest. Understanding it helps you make sense of what you feel during a hold, why the first holds of a session feel different from the later ones, and why some apneists cool their face before a static attempt.

What the dive reflex actually is

The dive reflex, or diving response, is a coordinated set of changes that the nervous system triggers when you stop breathing, and more strongly when your face is in cold water1. Its function is simple: when no new oxygen is coming in, slow down the rate at which the body spends what it has, and keep that oxygen for the organs that cannot do without it, the brain and the heart2.

Two branches of the autonomic nervous system work at the same time, which is unusual. The parasympathetic side, through the vagus nerve, slows the heart. The sympathetic side tightens blood vessels in the skin, the limbs and many internal organs. Normally these two systems pull in opposite directions; during a dive they cooperate.

Three effects, one goal

Bradycardia: the heart slows down

The best-known sign of the reflex is a drop in heart rate during the hold, called bradycardia. It develops over the first part of the breath-hold rather than instantly, then tends to settle at a lower level2. A slower heart does less work and therefore consumes less oxygen. In humans the slowing is usually moderate, and its size varies considerably between individuals and from one day to the next.

Peripheral vasoconstriction: blood moves to the core

At the same time, the small arteries supplying the skin, the muscles of the arms and legs and several abdominal organs narrow. Blood is redistributed towards the chest and the head, so the oxygen it carries goes preferentially to the brain and the heart muscle1. Tissues like resting muscle can tolerate low oxygen far better and rely on their own local reserves for a while. This narrowing is also why blood pressure rises during a long hold, and why hands and feet can feel cool or tingly afterwards.

Spleen contraction: a reserve of red blood cells

The spleen stores a concentrated volume of red blood cells. During breath-holds it contracts and releases part of this reserve into the circulation, slightly raising the blood's capacity to carry oxygen3. Unlike bradycardia, this effect builds over a series of holds rather than within a single one, and it gradually reverses once you return to normal breathing. It is one reason why many static athletes do a few progressively longer warm-up holds before a maximal attempt4.

The dive reflex does not give you more oxygen. It lets you spend the oxygen you have more slowly, and on the right organs.

What switches it on

Two stimuli trigger the response, and they add up2.

  • The breath-hold itself. Stopping breathing is enough to produce a mild response, even lying in bed with dry skin. This is the version you train in every dry static session.
  • Cold on the face. Receptors in the facial skin, carried by the trigeminal nerve, are particularly sensitive around the forehead, the eyes and the nose1. Cold water on this area while holding your breath typically produces a clearly stronger slowing of the heart than a hold in air. Colder water tends to give a stronger response, within limits.

Other factors modulate it. Anxiety and muscle tension work against it, which is one more reason relaxation matters so much in apnea. A calm, unhurried preparation, like the slow breathing described in our guide to breathwork for freediving, is the best starting point. Immersing the whole body without the face gives a weaker response than immersing the face itself.

Humans versus seals and whales

Marine mammals are the champions of this reflex. In seals the heart can slow dramatically during a dive, blood flow to large parts of the body is almost shut down, and the spleen is proportionally enormous, acting as a large reservoir of red blood cells. Their muscles are also loaded with myoglobin, an oxygen-storing protein.

Humans share the same basic machinery, but in a much milder form3. Our heart slows less, our spleen is small, and our muscles store far less oxygen. Even so, the human response is large enough to matter: in trained breath-hold divers, a more pronounced diving response has been associated with longer breath-holds4. It is one of several factors, alongside lung volume, tolerance to carbon dioxide and the ability to stay relaxed.

Can you train it?

The honest answer is: to some extent, and the evidence is still limited. Cross-sectional comparisons show that experienced breath-hold divers often display stronger responses than untrained people4. That alone does not prove that training caused the difference, since people with a naturally strong reflex may also be more drawn to the sport and better at it.

✓
What the Engan study measured

Engan and colleagues followed people new to apnea through two weeks of daily breath-hold training. They measured breath-hold duration, the heart-rate response during apnea, spleen contraction, and markers of red blood cell production (erythropoiesis), before and after the training period5.

It is often cited as evidence that parts of the response can adapt quickly. Keep its scope in mind: a small group of novices, a short protocol, and variables that did not all change in the same way. It tells you that the system is responsive to training, not how much it keeps improving over years.

In practice, regular breath-hold training appears to be the main lever, and you are already pulling it every time you do a CO2 table or a static session. Part of what improves is not the reflex itself but your ability to stay relaxed enough to let it work. Genetics also sets a range: two people on the same programme may respond quite differently.

Using it in dry static training

Because cold on the face strengthens the response, a common practice among static apneists is to cool the face just before a hold. It is not magic, and not everyone notices a difference, but many people report feeling calmer and more settled. If you want to experiment, keep it simple and keep it dry.

  1. Prepare a cold, damp clothCool tap water is enough. Avoid ice directly on the skin and avoid very cold water if you are not used to it.
  2. Lie down and breathe calmly for a few minutesSlow, relaxed breathing with a long exhale. No hyperventilation.
  3. Place the cloth over the forehead and eyesThis is the most sensitive area. Leave the nose and mouth completely free.
  4. Hold as usual and compareKeep the same table or routine and note how the hold feels, for instance when your first contraction arrives. Judge it over several sessions, not one.

You do not need to feel your pulse during a hold, and the Anima Apnea app does not measure heart rate. A better marker is how relaxed you feel and the timing of your first contraction, which the app records when you tap it. If cooling your face helps, it will show up there over a few weeks.

The reflex also explains a familiar pattern: the first hold of a session often feels harder than the third or fourth. Warm-up holds give the spleen and the cardiovascular response time to engage. Our guide to breaking the 3-minute barrier shows how to place warm-ups before a maximal attempt.

Safety first

!
Never hold your breath with your face in water on your own

Do not do static holds with your face in a basin, a sink or a bath. A blackout can happen without warning, and with your face in water it can be fatal. Cool the face with a damp cloth, lying down on dry land. Any breath-hold in water requires a trained buddy watching you the whole time.

  • The reflex does not prevent blackout. It slows the fall of oxygen; it does not stop it. The same rules apply as in any apnea training.
  • Never hyperventilate before a hold. It removes your warning signal without adding any useful oxygen.
  • Stop the session if you feel dizzy, notice palpitations, tingling, visual disturbances or anything unusual.
  • Irregular heartbeats have been documented during breath-hold diving3. If you have a heart condition or rhythm disorder, high blood pressure, a lung condition, epilepsy, or if you are pregnant, ask a doctor before breath-hold training or cold-water face exposure.
  • For anything in open water, learn with a certified course (AIDA, Molchanovs, SSI, PADI Freediver or equivalent). In the water, the reflex is only one part of a much larger picture that includes equalization and rescue skills.

Frequently asked questions

Does the dive reflex work out of the water?

Yes. Holding your breath alone triggers a mild version of it, even lying on a bed. Cooling the face, especially the forehead and the area around the eyes, usually makes it stronger.

Can you train the dive reflex?

To some extent. Trained divers tend to show a more pronounced response, and short training studies in beginners have measured changes in some of its components. How much each person adapts varies a lot, and genetics plays a part.

Does cold water on the face improve a static hold?

Many freedivers feel calmer and hold a little longer after cooling their face, and the physiology makes that plausible. The effect is individual and modest. Use a cold damp cloth while lying down, never hold your breath with your face in a basin or bath.

Is the dive reflex dangerous?

For healthy people it is a normal protective response. It does not prevent blackout, and in some people it can be accompanied by irregular heartbeats. Anyone with a heart condition should ask a doctor before breath-hold training or cold-water face exposure.

Why does my heart slow down when I hold my breath?

The vagus nerve slows the heart as part of the dive reflex. A slower heart uses less oxygen, and together with constricted blood vessels in the limbs it helps keep oxygen available for the brain and the heart itself.
